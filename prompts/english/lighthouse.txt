The lighthouse on the northern cape had not been lit for eleven years when Mara arrived with a toolbox, a borrowed van, and a stubborn idea. The town council called it a waste of money. The fishermen called it a ghost. Mara called it a machine that had simply stopped being asked to do its job.

She spent the first week cleaning. Salt had crept into every hinge and gear, and the great lens, a beehive of glass prisms taller than she was, wore a grey film that took four days of patient work to lift. By the second week she understood the clockwork that once turned the lens: a falling weight, a governor of spinning brass balls, and a chain of gears that converted a slow descent into a steady rotation of exactly one turn every twenty seconds.

The fishermen began to stop by in the evenings. At first they only watched. Then one brought a can of oil, another a box of old bulbs he had kept in his shed for no reason he could explain. On the last night of the month, when the weight began to fall and the beam swept across the black water for the first time in more than a decade, nobody said anything at all. They just stood on the rocks and counted the seconds between flashes, the way their fathers had taught them.
